6.2 APPENDIX II: DETAILED MATHEMATICAL DERIVATIONS

To simulate a deterministic environment that avoids unbounded extrapolation, the GaiaNet Earth Digital Twin leverages a series of bounded mathematical derivations. This appendix expands upon the primary formulas governing the Causal Cascade Engine and the Sustainability Health Index (SHI).

1. Sustainability Health Index (SHI) Derivation

The SHI serves as a normalizing heuristic to map localized Air Quality Indexes (\(A_q\)) into a standardized \([0, 100]\) grading scale. To prevent negative scores under extreme pollution events, the function is sharply bounded utilizing a min-max clamping function.

Let \(\mathbb{S}\) represent the domain of all possible health scores. The index is defined as:

$$ SHI = \max\left(0, \min\left(100, 100 - \frac{A_q}{k}\right)\right) $$

Where the degradation constant \(k = 3\). This specific constant determines the decay rate of livability; specifically, an environmental zone reaches \(SHI = 0\) (Critical Failure) when \(A_q \geq 300\), aligning with WHO "Hazardous" classifications.

2. The Drought Propagation Function (\(P_d\))

The causal engine calculates the probability of severe drought (\(P_d\)) based on the inverse relationship between the 3-month localized rainfall average (\(\bar{R}_{3m}\)) and the current thermal anomaly (\(\Delta T\) or \(A_t\)).

The absolute baseline drought probability without precipitation is set to 80%. Precipitation reduces this baseline logarithmically, while thermal anomalies increase it linearly:

$$ P_d(A_t, \bar{R}_{3m}) = 80 - \left( \frac{\bar{R}_{3m}}{\alpha} \right) + \beta \cdot A_t $$

Substituting the engine's empirical constraints (\(\alpha = 1.5\), \(\beta = 5\)):

$$ P_d = \max\left(10, \min\left(95, 80 - \frac{\bar{R}_{3m}}{1.5} + 5 A_t \right)\right) $$

Derivation Note: The bounds \([10, 95]\) prevent absolute certainty (100%) or absolute impossibility (0%) in the simulation, preserving stochastic realism.

3. Wildfire Risk Multiplier (\(W_r\))

Wildfire risk represents a tertiary cascade effect, mathematically dependent on both the primary temperature anomaly and the secondary drought derivation (\(P_d\)).

Let \(W_r\) represent the risk heuristic. We derive the risk as a heavy weighting of temperature spikes combined with a smaller fraction of the sustained drought probability (since dry brush acts as fuel):

$$ W_r = \lambda(A_t) + \gamma(P_d) $$

Substituting the simulation coefficients (\(\lambda = 25\), \(\gamma = 0.4\)) and clamping bounds:

$$ W_r = \max\left(10, \min\left(98, 25 \cdot A_t + 0.4 \cdot P_d\right)\right) $$

This ensures that even without high temperatures, a region experiencing 90% drought probability will still hold a \(W_r \approx 36\%\) baseload risk.

4. Sinusoidal Seasonal NDVI Offset (\(\delta\))

To create a mathematically sound digital twin of the Earth's vegetation cycles without relying on bandwidth-heavy raster images, the model generates the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) locally.

The seasonal phase offset simulates the shifting of biomes based on Earth's axial tilt. Let \(m\) be the numerical month (\(1 \leq m \leq 12\)). For Northern Temperate Latitudes (\(30^\circ < \phi < 70^\circ\)), the peak vegetation occurs in July (\(m=7\)). We model this as a sine wave with amplitude 0.2:

$$ \delta_{N}(\phi, m) = 0.2 \cdot \sin\left((m - \tau) \frac{\pi}{6}\right) $$

Where the phase shift \(\tau = 4\). By shifting the sine wave forward by 4 months, the maximum positive amplitude exactly aligns with \(m=7\) (July), and the maximum negative amplitude aligns with \(m=1\) (January).

For the Southern Hemisphere (\(-70^\circ < \phi < -30^\circ\)), the phase is inverted:

$$ \delta_{S}(\phi, m) = 0.2 \cdot \sin\left((m + 2) \frac{\pi}{6}\right) $$

The final global NDVI is therefore a composite of the base biome scalar (\(N_{base}\)), the seasonal offset (\(\delta\)), and uniform spatial noise (\(\epsilon \sim U[-0.05, 0.05]\)):

$$ NDVI_{final} = N_{base} + \delta_{\{\phi\}} + \epsilon $$
